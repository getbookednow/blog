A portfolio isn't a gallery. It isn't a mood board or a record of everything you've made. A portfolio is the brief a client uses to decide whether to hire you. Most creative portfolios fail that brief — not because the work is wrong, but because the presentation is built for the wrong person.

I've watched this from both sides. As a model, I sat across from casting directors opening comp cards and websites with a specific question in mind: does this person fit the brief? The decision happened in seconds. Working now with creatives on their digital presence, I see the same dynamic from the other side — the instant judgement a potential client makes when they land on a site. Most portfolios are built for the creative who made them. The client who needs to hire from them is an afterthought.

What a client actually needs to see

When someone lands on your portfolio with a brief to fill, they're moving through three questions fast:

  1. Is this person's work the right level? Image quality and selection answer part of this. But visible credits answer it more efficiently. One recognisable client name does more work than thirty beautiful personal projects. Your strongest commercial work should be impossible to miss — not buried behind personal or experimental work you're more attached to.
  2. Do I understand what this person specialises in? "Photographer" is not a speciality. "Fashion photographer specialising in commercial campaigns and editorial — SA, Asia, and Europe" is. If a visitor can't identify your lane within ten seconds, they move on. Not because they're impatient. Because they have a decision to make and you haven't helped them make it.
  3. Can I contact them right now, easily? An email on a contact page two clicks away costs bookings. Your direct contact — email, WhatsApp, or agent — should be visible without hunting. The client choosing between three creatives will take the path of least resistance.

The mistakes that come up most

Loading slowly. A site that takes more than a few seconds to load on mobile loses a significant portion of visitors before they see a single image. Most creative sites are image-heavy with no optimisation. This is fixable — but you have to know it's happening.

No hierarchy. Everything weighted equally means nothing stands out. Lead with the work that signals the level and type of client you want more of — not the most recent work, not the work you're most emotionally invested in.

Images without context. A stunning image with no caption, no client name, no year — it's beautiful, but it's not working for you. Every image should tell someone unfamiliar with your work what it is, who it was for, and when. That context is proof of work, and proof of work is what converts interest into a booking.

Looking like everyone else. There are a handful of portfolio templates the majority of fashion creatives seem to have landed on. If your site looks like the one the client saw ten minutes ago, you've already started from a disadvantage. Distinctive doesn't require complicated — it requires considered.
