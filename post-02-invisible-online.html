The most common thing I hear: "I have a website and I post consistently. Why isn't it working?" Having a digital presence and having a functional digital presence are two different things. The gap between them is quiet — and it costs real work.

When I look at a creative's digital footprint — actually look, not glance — the same gaps come up every time. The work is good. The instinct is there. But the way it all fits together — or doesn't — is broken in ways the person can't see from the inside, because they already know what they mean to say. The problem is that the outside world doesn't.

The five gaps I find every time

  1. Your website exists but doesn't describe you A portfolio full of images communicates a lot to someone who looks carefully. It communicates almost nothing to a search system — or a client scanning quickly under time pressure. If the text on your site doesn't explicitly state your discipline, your market, your credits, and your location, you're relying entirely on people spending time they don't have. Most don't.
  2. Your credits live in your head, not online You've worked with recognisable clients. You've shot campaigns people have seen. But where is that written down — in plain text, attached to your name, on a page that persists? A PDF tearsheet doesn't count. A caption from two years ago doesn't count. Credits need to exist somewhere structured and findable.
  3. Your bio doesn't do any work "Fashion photographer based in Cape Town" is a placeholder, not a bio. It doesn't communicate your speciality, your level, the kind of briefs you take, or why someone should call you specifically over anyone else. A bio should apply to exactly one person. Most apply to fifty.
  4. Your platforms tell different stories Your Instagram says one thing. Your website says something slightly different. Your LinkedIn — if you have one — is years out of date. Inconsistency isn't just confusing to a human reader. It reduces your credibility to any system trying to build a picture of who you are. Consistency is a signal, not an aesthetic preference.
  5. You don't appear when someone searches your specialty Try this: search your city, your discipline, and the type of client you want. If you're not on the first page of results, that's a structure problem, not a talent problem. These are fixable — but only once you know they exist.

Why follower count doesn't fix this

Social following measures human attention. It tells people who browse that other people find your work worth following. It doesn't tell a client scanning for the right person — quickly, under pressure — what you specialise in, who you've worked with, or whether you're the right fit for a specific brief.

A creative with a modest following and a well-structured presence — clear discipline, named credits, consistent description across platforms — will consistently outperform a much larger account that's image-heavy and text-light. Every time. The structure is what makes you findable. The following is what happens after.

The right question to ask

Not "do I have a digital presence?" but: if someone who has never heard of you searched for what you do right now — would they find you, and would what they find accurately represent the level you're operating at? Most creatives, if they're honest, would answer no to at least one part of that.

Where to start

Don't rebuild everything at once. Start with understanding what's actually broken before spending time or money fixing anything. I've seen creatives redo their entire website when the real issue was something much simpler. I've seen others invest in new photography when the problem was their existing work had no written context around it at all.

Diagnosis before build. That's always the right order.
