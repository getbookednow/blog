What a new signee needs before their first booking.

Most agencies sign talent and move straight to pitching. The infrastructure that makes that talent findable and credible to the market gets built later — if at all. That gap costs bookings from day one, and it compounds.

The gap that opens on day one

A new signee joins your agency. They're talented — that's why you signed them. You know what they can do. You've seen the work. You understand where they fit in the market.

The problem is that the market doesn't know any of that yet. And in the time between signing and first booking, the window for establishing how they're perceived is open — and most agencies don't use it.

What gets established in the first 30 days of a creative's agency relationship tends to persist for years. The positioning that forms in that window — how they describe themselves, how their presence reads, what their first point of contact communicates — becomes the baseline. Changing it later is harder than getting it right at the start.

What most new signees arrive with

A portfolio that's been assembled for a previous version of their career. A bio written for a different market, or no bio at all. An Instagram profile that's a mix of personal and professional with no clear discipline stated. No owned domain. Credits scattered across platforms in inconsistent formats, or not online at all.

Sometimes a Linktree. Occasionally a PDF. Rarely a site that would pass a 10-second casting director test.

This isn't a criticism of the creative — they were hired to produce work, not to build digital infrastructure. But it means that from day one, the agency is presenting talent to the market through a presence that was built for a different stage of that talent's career. That gap costs bookings, quietly, from the beginning.

What a new signee actually needs before their first booking

A positioning brief. One document that defines: their discipline in plain language, their market (who hires them and for what), their strongest credits, the aesthetic they're known for, and how they're differentiated from similar talent on the roster. This brief becomes the source of truth for every piece of copy written about them — bio, agency profile, site, LinkedIn.

Consistent naming and bio across platforms. Same name, same spelling, same stated discipline on Instagram, LinkedIn, the agency site, and any casting platforms they're registered on. Inconsistency fragments their digital footprint and undermines discoverability.

An owned domain. Not a Linktree. Not a social profile. A URL with their name on it that leads to a portfolio that works. This is the one thing that makes everything else findable — the anchor point that search engines and AI systems use to establish who this person is.

Text-based credits. Their best credits need to exist in writing, somewhere indexed and findable. Not in a PDF. Not in a caption. On a page that can be searched, read, and referenced by anyone looking at them for the first time.

The question to ask

For every new signee: if a casting director in another market searched their name right now, what would they find — and would it accurately represent what this talent can do at the level we're placing them? If the answer is anything other than yes — the infrastructure isn't ready.

Why agencies don't do this consistently

Capacity. Onboarding a new signee while managing an existing roster, running bookings, and maintaining client relationships leaves no bandwidth for a structured digital infrastructure process. It's not a priority failure — it's a systems failure. There's no process for it because there's never been a dedicated resource to own it.
